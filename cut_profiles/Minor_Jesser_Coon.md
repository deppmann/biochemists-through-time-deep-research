**Minor Jesser Coon (1921-2018)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The laboratory at Urbana--Champaign smells faintly chemical and unmistakably wrong. Beakers line the benches, chalk dust coats the blackboard, and Minor Jesser Coon stands thinner than he should be, spooning down a purified synthetic meal he helped make himself. It is wartime, and he and his fellow graduate students are both researchers and raw material, consuming diets deliberately stripped of essential amino acids, week after week, to find out what the human body truly needs. The taste is vile. The fatigue is constant. At some point, looking pale and hollowed out, Coon earns a remark from a colleague riffing on a new Broadway musical: "Pore Jud is daid." The nickname sticks.

The tension is not theatrical but real. These experiments would never pass a modern ethics board. The chemicals are unrefined; the safeguards minimal. Yet the stakes feel absolute. William C. Rose's lab is trying to pin down the molecular grammar of nutrition itself, quantities, not guesses, and the work demands bodies willing to endure uncertainty. For Coon, this crucible forges habits that never leave him: tolerance for discomfort, skepticism of shortcuts, and an understanding that biology resists clean answers.

Years later, those habits surface again, this time on a Philadelphia commuter train. Now a young faculty member, Coon studies how amino acids break down into short-chain fatty acids. One intermediate, isovaleric acid, smells by his own account like dirty tennis shoes. He carries it with him, sometimes literally, earning wary looks from fellow passengers. The social isolation barely registers. He is following metabolism downstream, from diet to chemistry, and the trail leads to molecules like HMG-CoA, a fleeting junction that quietly governs cholesterol synthesis. The pattern is set: endure the unpleasant parts to get at the truth.

Coon eventually articulates this philosophy with characteristic bluntness. "Purification of an enzyme is not a scientific end but only a beginning," he tells audiences, a line he repeats for decades. The remark is less aphorism than instruction. Isolating something cleanly, he believes, only earns the right to ask harder questions about how it works, with whom, and under what conditions.

That belief reaches its sharpest expression in Ann Arbor in 1968. Late at night, Coon and postdoc Anthony Lu step into a neighboring lab to borrow a spectrophotometer their own group lacks. On the screen appears a peak at 450 nanometers, the signature of cytochrome P450, confirming that a membrane-bound enzyme system, long thought too fragile to reassemble, can function outside its native cellular home. It is a quiet act of trespass and a loud rebuttal to scientific orthodoxy.

At its core, the chemistry is deceptively simple. Cytochrome P450 enzymes insert one atom of oxygen into oily, reluctant molecules, fatty acids, drugs, hydrocarbons, while reducing the other atom to water. To do this, they need partners: an electron-donating reductase and the right phospholipid membrane to bring the pieces together. Coon's reconstitution shows that context matters as much as components; enzymes are not soloists but ensembles. This discovery unlocked pharmacology: P450 enzymes metabolize roughly half of all medicines in clinical use, determining whether a drug is activated or inactivated in the body. Understanding Coon's reconstituted system revealed why genetic variation in P450 expression makes some patients rapid metabolizers and others slow, explaining drug efficacy gaps and adverse reactions. The field of pharmacogenomics built on this foundation.

As his lab grows, so does his sense of responsibility for the people in it. Former students remember him as a demanding but generous mentor who insisted on clear writing and modeled how to balance a scientific career with a life outside it. Mentorship, for Coon, is another metabolic process, energy passed along with structure and restraint.

Decades after those Urbana diets, he runs five-kilometer races into his late eighties, still lean, still disciplined. The image echoes the opening scene: a body subjected to stress, not for punishment, but for understanding. In Coon's world, science advances because someone is willing to stay in the room, endure the smell, and make space for the next person at the bench.
