**Wolfram Saenger (1939-2026)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The drawer opens. Inside are hundreds of pages, typed, annotated, carefully ordered, an entire account of how DNA is supposed to work. Wolfram Saenger sits at his desk at the Institute for Crystallography in Berlin, flipping through a manuscript he finished in 1976 and never published. The pages are intact, the logic careful. Outside the lab, the field is accelerating. Inside the drawer is a version of science that no longer holds. As he reads, the problem becomes unavoidable. The manuscript assumes DNA is uniform, right-handed, predictable. But in the four years since he wrote it, left-handed Z-DNA has appeared, synthetic DNA fragments can now be built to order, and single-crystal structures are replacing averaged fiber patterns. The text is not unfinished, it is conceptually wrong. "It often appeared to me that I was trying to catch a running train," Saenger writes later, as research on DNA structure begins "booming." The risk is quiet but real: publish something obsolete, or discard years of work. He discards it. Saenger abandons the draft entirely and begins again, rebuilding the book around DNA polymorphism, hydration, and sequence-dependent structure. The choice delays publication by years, but it produces Principles of Nucleic Acid Structure, a work that treats DNA as a responsive molecule shaped by water, salt, and geometry rather than a static scaffold. "The whole story had to be rewritten," he explains in the preface, framing the loss not as failure but as accuracy. That same patience defines his later work on photosynthesis. In the late 1980s, Saenger's laboratory turns to Photosystem I, a membrane protein complex so large it resists conventional crystallography. Progress comes slowly: a six-angstrom outline, then four angstroms, then years of refinement. The lab processes dark green bands of chlorophyll-rich membranes, purified with detergent and separated by centrifugation. Thirteen years pass before the structure resolves at near-atomic detail. There is no dramatic breakthrough, only sustained attention. The science behind the effort is elemental. Photosystem I is a nanoscale solar engine, a disk of protein holding nearly a hundred chlorophyll molecules arranged so energy can move efficiently toward a reaction center. Saenger's structure shows how that arrangement makes the process almost lossless. In parallel work on Photosystem II, his group maps the metal cluster, four manganese ions and one calcium, that splits water into oxygen, protons, and electrons. Oxygen-based life depends on that architecture being precise. Saenger's focus extends to what crystallography often ignores. Hydrogen atoms, nearly invisible to X-rays, matter deeply to him. He argues publicly that neutron diffraction, slower and far more difficult, will be required to understand water oxidation fully. The concern traces back to his early rules for nucleic acids: at least two hydrogen bonds are needed to hold a base pair flat. Structure, for Saenger, is enforced by constraint. The drawer scene endures because it captures his method. Rather than defend an outdated account, he removes it. Rather than accept partial vision, he seeks a better tool. The train keeps moving. Saenger keeps rewriting.
