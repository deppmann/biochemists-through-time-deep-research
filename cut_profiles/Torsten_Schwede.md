**Torsten Schwede (1967-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Torsten Schwede is standing over a crystallography data set in a lab at the University of Freiburg, watching as another enzyme fails to behave as it should. The enzyme, histidine ammonia-lyase, has been purified, prepared, and coaxed, but it will not crystallize cleanly enough to reveal its structure. Instead, it clumps together. This is the kind of roadblock that can stall a dissertation for months.

But Schwede does not give up on the enzyme. He thinks about the protein sequence again, this time focusing on one surface cysteine residue that he thinks may be causing the clumping. He changes this cysteine residue to alanine. The enzyme crystallizes. When he finally solves the enzyme's structure, he finds something unexpected: an internal chemical feature made not from vitamin or metal cofactors but from the enzyme itself. Three amino acids have rearranged themselves to form an unknown cofactor with electrophilic properties. This is the key to the enzyme's chemistry, done without any outside help. This is Schwede's first major scientific contribution, but it is also the first of many that will follow the same pattern: the search for structure in unexpected places.

This is the kind of thinking that will carry Schwede far beyond the laboratory. By the early 2000s, he is at the University of Basel, working at the intersection of biochemistry and computation. The cost of structural data is too high, while protein sequences are proliferating. Schwede is part of the team that builds the SWISS-MODEL server, one of the first automated web servers that can generate a three-dimensional protein structure from an amino acid sequence. His collaborators praise his quiet insistence that tools should be democratized, made free and accessible to researchers everywhere, not locked behind paywalls. One can input a protein sequence and retrieve the predicted structure as output. By 2002, tens of thousands of protein models are being generated annually.

The science behind this is simple but powerful. Proteins that have similar sequences have similar shapes. So if one's protein can be modeled and compared. The innovation that Schwede brought to this field was not the method itself but the automation of this method. This means that protein structure prediction no longer requires special knowledge. This innovation has major implications though for biochemists. It allows hypotheses about protein function, binding, and mechanisms to be tested long before an experiment succeeds or even before it falls.

It's been many years since Schwede's work in protein structure prediction, and he's now faced with yet another revolution in how proteins are structured. This time, artificial intelligence systems like AlphaFold are predicting protein structures with unprecedented accuracy. Instead of fighting this revolution, Schwede incorporates this new tool into his work. He and his team are using millions of structures that predicted by AI to identify previously unknown protein families. In fact, they've identified a completely new protein fold. When viewed, this protein fold looks like a flower. While this work is obviously on a much grander scale than his initial work with enzymes, the question remains the same: what's inside this protein structure?

Schwede understands the limits of this new tool. Even now, researchers can predict three-dimensional protein structures with remarkable efficiency, yet still do not fully understand, in mechanistic detail, how the AI arrives at those predictions, nor how a protein actually folds itself in the first place. While the tools have become more refined, the underlying question of protein folding and structure remains the same.
