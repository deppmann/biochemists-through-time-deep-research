**Alan Fersht (1943-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

It is spring, 1990, and the benches in the Chemical Laboratory at Cambridge are polished to an impractical shine. Alan Fersht sits a prospective PhD student down in front of an Evans & Sutherland graphics workstation, a rare, glowing altar of computation in a world still dominated by wet glassware. On the screen floats a three-dimensional model of Barnase, a bacterial enzyme that is lethal to its host if not kept in check. Fersht is charming, engaging in what feels like a light-hearted chat, but the test is already underway. He watches how the student interacts with the rotating lattice of atoms. In this lab, visualization is not just aesthetic; it is the prerequisite for intervention.

Fersht is operating at the frontier of a new discipline: protein engineering. For decades, biochemists could only observe enzymes as they appeared in nature. Fersht wants to rewrite them. But biology is noisy, wet, and often maddeningly imprecise, while Fersht, a former chess champion who maintains a scholarly obsession with antique Jaques Staunton sets, demands the clarity of a grandmaster. He requires a board where every piece moves according to fixed, quantifiable rules.

Knowing how things work, down to the level of individual atoms, is the simple maxim that belies the aggressive rigor he applies to the molecular world. To understand how a protein folds, how a chaotic string of amino acids snaps into a functional 3D shape, he approaches it like a game of chess. You cannot understand the strategy by staring at a static board; you must remove a piece and see if the game falls apart.

This logic births his signature method: Φ-value analysis. By systematically mutating specific amino acids, deleting a chemical interaction here, severing a hydrogen bond there, he measures how the change affects the speed of folding. It is a way to map the invisible. If removing a specific amino acid slows the process, that part of the protein must be formed at the crucial "transition state," the molecular top-of-the-hill before the structure slides into place. Fersht effectively turns the protein's sequence into a coordinate system, plotting the energetic landscape of life's most fundamental self-assembly.

This insistence on mathematical truth makes him a formidable gatekeeper. Years later, when a former student, Victor Muñoz, publishes papers claiming a protein called BBL folds "downhill", without the energetic barrier Fersht's model predicts, Fersht does not politely disagree. He dismantles the claim. In a series of papers, he argues that the "downhill" result is an artifact of aggregation and probe choice, a mirage caused by flawed assumptions and analysis of the data.

It is a public, bruising dispute, but for Fersht, it is necessary hygiene. In a field seduced by parsimonious theories, he insists on the raw friction of data. He stands in the polished lab, holding the line between a pretty model and physical reality, ensuring that when the pieces move, they follow the rules.
