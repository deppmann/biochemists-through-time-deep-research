**Irving M. London (1918-2018)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Irving M. London's career ran on two tracks that eventually converged: building institutions and probing the molecular logic of the blood. In 1954, at age thirty-six, he was chosen as founding chair of the Department of Medicine at the newly established Albert Einstein College of Medicine, where he also directed medical services at Bronx Municipal Hospital. It was a job with no template. He had to recruit faculty, set clinical protocols, and define research priorities for an institution that existed mostly on paper, all while a city hospital strained under the needs of the Bronx. He led the department until 1970.

London had come to medicine by way of the Army. During World War II he served as a captain in the Medical Corps, studying chloroquine as an antimalarial drug, and afterward he was posted to Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands to serve as a physician during the postwar atomic bomb tests. That practical, applied cast of mind stayed with him in the laboratory. At Einstein, and later in his own research group, London turned to the question of how red blood cells know when to make hemoglobin. Working with cultured erythroid cells, his group identified a kinase, now known as the heme-regulated inhibitor (HRI), that slows protein synthesis when heme is scarce by phosphorylating a translation initiation factor. The effect is a feedback loop: globin chains are not produced faster than the heme needed to complete them becomes available. It was one of the clearest early demonstrations that a small metabolite could directly govern the machinery of protein synthesis, work that helped establish the broader principle of metabolite-dependent translational control.

In 1970 London took on a second founding role, becoming the first director of the Harvard-MIT Program in Health Sciences and Technology (HST), a position he held until 1985 and the first to formally join the two institutions under one professorship. He continued teaching for decades afterward. Across a career that ran from wartime service in the Pacific to building two academic programs from scratch to bench work on hemoglobin regulation, London's throughline was a persistent curiosity about how the body senses scarcity and responds to it, whether the scarce resource was hospital beds, faculty lines, or a single molecule of heme.
