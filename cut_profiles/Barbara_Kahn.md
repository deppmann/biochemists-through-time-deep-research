**Barbara B. Kahn (dates unverified)**

*Profile by Maggie Shifflett, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Barbara Kahn was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes as a teenager growing up in Fairway, Kansas, in an era when nothing about managing the disease was fast. There was no home glucose meter and no hemoglobin A1c test; without an emergency, a blood sugar order could take days to come back, by which time she had lost all context for what she had eaten or how she had exercised. The gap between her physiology and its measurement was maddening. Years later, as a Stanford undergraduate studying abroad in Fiesole, Italy, she walked into a local phlebotomy lab near her bus stop expecting the same delay, and instead had blood drawn that day and a result the next morning. It was, she later said, an eye-opening glimpse of how much more patient-oriented medicine could be.

That gap between disease and its explanation became Kahn's scientific target. Her path there was not a straight line: as a medical student she leaned toward family medicine until a mentor, Dan Federman, told her she liked to understand things in depth and would be happier in internal medicine. An endocrine fellowship followed, one she had originally hoped to avoid because diabetes already loomed so large in her own life. At the National Institutes of Health, she traded clinical rotations for radioactive isotopes and subcellular fractionation, using biochemical tools to track the molecular handshakes that govern glucose uptake.

The breakthrough came through an unexpected discovery in a mouse model. Researchers had engineered mice (AG4OX) that expressed elevated levels of a glucose transporter in their adipose tissue. The mice became obese, storing huge amounts of fat, yet remained insulin-sensitive. Normal biology suggested obesity should trigger insulin resistance: fat cells clogged with triglycerides should fail to respond to the hormone. Instead, these animals paradoxically remained metabolically healthy. Kahn realized the fat tissue itself was generating protective signals.

The biochemical basis emerged through careful molecular analysis. GLUT4, the insulin-responsive glucose transporter, shuttles between intracellular vesicles and the plasma membrane. When insulin binds its receptor, a cascade of phosphorylation events, mediated by IRS proteins and PI3 kinase, drives GLUT4-containing endosomes to the cell surface, allowing glucose to enter. Kahn discovered that adipose tissue with high GLUT4 expression produced signals that preserved insulin signaling in distant tissues. The fat cells were not just storing fuel; they were secreting metabolic orchestrators.

In 2014, using high-resolution mass spectrometry, Kahn's team identified a cluster of lipids elevated nearly 18-fold in the metabolically healthy obese mice. These were previously unknown molecules, later named FAHFAs (fatty acid esters of hydroxy fatty acids). Lipids are not mere energy storage; they are signaling molecules. FAHFAs activate GPR120, a G-protein-coupled receptor on macrophages and intestinal cells, suppressing inflammation and improving whole-body glucose homeostasis. A single biochemical discovery, identifying an entirely new class of lipid metabolites, showed how adipose tissue orchestrates systemic metabolism through lipid signaling.

Decades after that phlebotomy lab in Fiesole, Kahn had transformed the invisible chemistry of her own diabetes into a named mechanism. The signal was no longer unknown. The message was clear: metabolic health emerges not from isolated organs but from coordinated lipid signaling between tissues.
