**Peter J. Ratcliffe (1954-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

On August 5, 1992, at the Nuffield Department of Medicine in Oxford, Peter J. Ratcliffe holds a letter from the journal *Nature*. The paper he submitted concerns the genetic response to hypoxia, how a cell registers that oxygen has run short. Dr. Rory Howlett, the editor, writes with polite finality. One reviewer is favorable. The other, the letter reports, is "not persuaded" that the findings "represent a sufficient advance in our understanding of the mechanisms of genetic response to hypoxia to justify publication in *Nature*."

Ratcliffe does not discard the letter. He files it away, a quiet record of what it costs to move against the grain. The resistance is a familiar companion. As a schoolboy in Lancashire he had found himself in a similar position of redirected momentum, summoned in 1971 to a headmaster's study and told, by an austere man in an academic gown, that he would study medicine instead of the chemistry he loved. "I followed him out of the classroom. He said, 'Ratcliffe, I think you should study medicine.' He wasn't the sort of guy you challenged. So I said, 'Yes, sir.' That's what we did. We struck out chemistry and wrote medicine on the university forms," Ratcliffe recalls. He then approached his medical examinations with a calculated "brutality," seeking the academic standing that would buy him his own professional freedom.

That strategic cast of mind defines his laboratory life. Years earlier he had wrestled with the "physico-chemical properties" of myoglobin in isolated perfused kidneys, pumping enormous concentrations of the protein through the organs and seeing nothing happen. He abandoned the project rather than be held by an insoluble distraction, and moved from the crude resolution of 1980s magnets into molecular biology. When he and his collaborator Chris Pugh took the enhancer that switches on the erythropoietin gene and inserted it into ordinary connective tissue cells, cells with no business making erythropoietin at all, they expected silence. Instead the cells sensed low oxygen and turned the gene on. The discoveries, Ratcliffe has said, "were not sheer brilliance. They were stumbling around and eventually recognising that something that had been actually almost obvious was there before you." What had been there all along was this: every cell in the body is an oxygen sensor.

The machinery behind the response works like a thermostat that runs by destroying its own parts. When oxygen is present, an enzyme called prolyl hydroxylase attaches a hydroxyl group to a protein called HIF-1α. The tag is a death sentence: it is recognized by the von Hippel-Lindau protein, which marks HIF-1α for the cell's disposal system, and the protein is shredded as fast as it is made. When oxygen falls, the enzyme loses the substrate it needs and stops tagging. HIF-1α survives, accumulates, and moves into the nucleus, where it switches on hundreds of genes that help the body adapt, among them the genes that call for more red blood cells.

By 2001 the race to identify that tagging enzyme had reached a fever pitch. A colleague burst into Ratcliffe's office saying, "here's your gene!" and the last link in the circuit was in hand. For Ratcliffe the beauty of the system lies in its apparent wastefulness, a Rube Goldberg device of ceaseless creation and destruction that buys a near-instantaneous response to a breath of thin air. In 2019 the work brought him a share of the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

He keeps the rejection letter on his desk, thirty years on, filed beside his notebook on oxygen sensing. When young scientists ask him how long discovery takes, he pulls it out and shows them the date. It is a bench mark: the narrow question nobody wanted became the foundation that now rescues patients whose cells are gasping for breath.
