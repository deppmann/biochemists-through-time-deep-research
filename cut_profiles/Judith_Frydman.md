**Judith Frydman (1963-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The laboratory at Memorial Sloan Kettering smells faintly of rabbit blood. Judith Frydman stands at the bench in Jim Rothman's newly established department, pipetting fractions of reticulocyte lysate, the cellular machinery from a million rabbit red blood cells, stripped down to its essential parts. She's hunting for something most of her colleagues believe doesn't exist: a eukaryotic version of the bacterial protein-folding machine GroEL. The cytoplasm of a mammalian cell is a molecular crowd, 300 milligrams of protein packed into every milliliter, and if a newly made protein can't fold correctly in that crush, it will clump into a useless tangle. Somewhere in these tubes is the machine that prevents the disaster. The stakes are conceptual as much as technical. For thirty years, biochemistry has lived by Christian Anfinsen's elegant demonstration: unfold a small protein in a test tube, remove the chemical denaturant, and the protein refolds itself. No help needed. But Frydman suspects the textbook story is a lie, or at least, a lab artifact. Real cells don't work that way.

When the electron microscopy images come back from Wolfgang Baumeister's lab, they show a barrel. Not the single ring of GroEL, but a double-barreled structure of roughly 1 million Daltons. It's TRiC, the TCP-1 Ring Complex, built from eight different subunits arranged in two stacked rings, and it can fold proteins like actin and tubulin that bacterial chaperones can't touch. Frydman's mentor Ulrich Hartl will later call her work "tour-de-force biochemistry," but in the moment, she's already thinking ahead. "Our work highlights how an incredibly elaborate network of chaperones controls and monitors every aspect of the life of proteins in the cell, from birth to death," she'll say years later. What she's discovered is that cells don't trust spontaneous folding. They supervise it. Here's the physics: a protein is a one-dimensional string of amino acids that must collapse into a precise three-dimensional shape to function. In a test tube with one protein molecule, this can happen on its own. In a cell with thousands of other proteins jostling for space, a half-folded chain will stick to anything nearby.

TRiC solves this by acting as a chamber, a barrel-shaped container that isolates the protein from the crowd and gives it a protected space to fold, then releases it only when the shape is correct. Frydman's career has been a series of visualizations: mapping the "trash cans" where cells sequester misfolded proteins (JUNQ and IPOD compartments), showing that the genetic code's redundant "letters" act as deliberate pause buttons to slow the ribosome at critical folding moments, and most recently, linking TRiC mutations to a new class of brain malformations in children, called "TRiCopathies." The chemistry student from Buenos Aires has spent her career excavating the ancient machinery buried in every human cell. Her work on TRiC complemented the studies of Manajit Hayer-Hartl, whose meticulous reconstitution of the bacterial GroEL/ES chaperonin system revealed the thermodynamic principles of assisted folding, principles that Frydman's eukaryotic discoveries extended into an entirely new domain of complexity. The eukaryotic chaperonin her colleagues had said could not exist now has a name in every pediatric neurology clinic that sequences for it; the subunits Frydman first coaxed out of a million rabbit red blood cells are the reason a clinician can sit with a parent and finally say what went wrong.
