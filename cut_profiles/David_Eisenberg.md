**David Eisenberg (1939-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In a Princeton office, David S. Eisenberg sits with the only kind of "specimen" a theorist can touch: coordinates. Columns of numbers, myoglobin's best-known three-dimensional positions, sparse by modern standards, promise an answer to a question that has already begun to haunt him: what does the hydrophobic interaction cost, energetically, inside a real protein? He has come to work with Walter Kauzmann, the scientist who helped name that interaction, and Eisenberg is trying to compute it anyway, to turn a physical intuition into a calculation. But the data won't hold still. The coordinates are not precise enough for the thermodynamic model he wants to build, and the plan fails in the most clinical way: not with an explosion, but with uncertainty.

That failure becomes a hinge. Eisenberg does not abandon the mathematics; he changes what he thinks mathematics needs. If the numbers are the problem, then someone has to make better numbers. He goes to Caltech to learn X-ray crystallography from Richard Dickerson, trading pure theory for a craft that can pin atoms down. It is an apprenticeship move, less a reinvention than an inheritance, carried forward from mentors who treated proteins as solvable physical objects. Years later, looking back on the turn from frustration to method, Eisenberg will describe the moment when his own ideas finally began to land: "At last I was able to get down to energetic calculations on a protein, and came up with the idea of the hydrophobic moment. This and related ideas gave me for the first time the feeling that I could make discoveries."

He keeps building the bridge between calculation and structure. On one side are sequences and principles, hydrophobicity, packing, the geometry of folds. On the other side are maps: electron density, diffraction patterns, assemblies that refuse to stay neatly monomeric. In the early 1990s at UCLA, a different kind of puzzle emerges while his group studies diphtheria toxin, a 535-residue protein. The electron density does something strange: a domain expected to fold back onto its own chain seems to occupy its partner's space instead. Out of that mismatch comes a mechanism and a name, "3D domain swapping", a way for proteins to exchange appendages and form higher-order complexes without fully unraveling.

Then the story tightens around a more stubborn material: amyloid. In 1999, Eisenberg makes a conscious decision to steer his laboratory toward prions and aggregation diseases, toward fibers that had long been visible under microscopes but structurally opaque. In 2005, the team confronts a practical humiliation: their amyloid-forming peptide doesn't grow into handsome crystals; it produces something closer to powder. They take it to Grenoble, to the European Synchrotron, and use micro-focused beams to read what ordinary X-rays can't. The reward is an interface, the "steric zipper", two β-sheets locked together so tightly they exclude water, a dry adhesive that can make misfolded protein assemblies terrifyingly stable. Eisenberg and his colleagues would later distill the lesson: the adhesive parts of these fiber-forming proteins turn out to be short segments, reducing a century of pathological clutter to a structural unit.

In simplest terms, his work insists that the forces that fold proteins can also harden them into something else. The hydrophobic effect drives non-polar residues to hide from water; the hydrophobic moment helps quantify how those residues distribute around an α-helix, whether a helix has one face suited for membranes and another for solvent. In amyloid, the same urge to exclude water becomes an architectural seal: side chains interdigitate like zipper teeth, creating a dry spine that favors growth.

The craft keeps shrinking its objects. In 2015, confronted with alpha-synuclein crystals smaller than the wavelength of visible light, invisible under an ordinary microscope, the lab turns to MicroED, electron diffraction, to map a toxic core that synchrotron X-rays can't easily handle. The lineage comes full circle: the theorist who once stared at inadequate coordinates now builds tools that manufacture coordinates for the nearly invisible. He keeps returning to the same wager, inherit a principle, then force it to face reality, until the hidden world gives up its geometry.
