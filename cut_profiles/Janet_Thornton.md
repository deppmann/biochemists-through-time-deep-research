**Janet Thornton (1949-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Janet Thornton stares at the official letterhead of a London hospital, the paper a heavy weight in her hands. In the clinical quiet of the National Institute for Medical Research at Mill Hill, the 22-year-old biophysicist feels like a ghost in a foreign land. Around her, colleagues trade rapid-fire jargon about cellular signaling and metabolic pathways, a dialect that remains stubbornly opaque to her physics-trained mind. For eighteen months, she has struggled to reconcile the precise, mathematical world of energy minima with the "messy" reality of biological molecules, her hands adjusting spectroscopic instruments to study dinucleotides that refuse to yield their secrets. The hospital job offer is a literal exit strategy, a chance to return to the comfortable certainty of clinical physics and abandon a PhD that feels like a slow-motion collision with a language barrier.

The tension is not a lack of aptitude, but a crisis of identity. In 1971, the bridge between hardcore physics and biology is a narrow, rickety scaffold. To her peers, she is a quantitative intruder; to herself, she is someone who "knew no biology" and lacks a coherent plan. She is aware that her chosen path is precarious; as she later noted, "computational biology was once described to me as 'the dustbin area of science' but this view has changed radically." If she takes the clinical post, she gains professional security but surrenders intellectual autonomy. She realizes that hospital physicists are often relegated to the status of technicians, skilled hands following a doctor's protocol rather than minds in command of their own inquiry.

Thornton declines the offer, choosing the chaos of the lab over the security of the ward. This decision marks a pivot from disorientation to a stubborn, pattern-seeking resolve. She decides that if she cannot speak the biologists' language, she will build a more logical one from the ground up. She begins treating protein structures as geometric puzzles rather than messy biological mysteries, looking past the individual atoms to find the universal rules of the fold.

This logic eventually manifests as CATH, a hierarchical system that treats the seemingly infinite variety of protein shapes as a finite, predictable vocabulary. Thornton realized that while nature can string amino acids into endless sequences, it only uses a few hundred basic "folds" to create functional machinery. Her system categorizes these domains by their secondary structure content, the Class, and their gross spatial arrangement, or Architecture. By mapping the Topology and Homologous superfamilies, she identifies recurring motifs, such as the nearly universal right-handed turn, that act as the modular "Lego bricks" of the biological universe.

The disorientation of Mill Hill has been replaced by the quiet satisfaction of the "simple pattern" found in the right-handed twist of a protein chain. In the 1980s, she logged those patterns by hand into a physical ledger known as the "Big Blue Book," pages that were the analog first draft of the searchable fold databases a drug designer now opens before shaping a molecule to fit its target. The physicist who had once felt like a ghost in a foreign land had written the grammar the whole field would read.
