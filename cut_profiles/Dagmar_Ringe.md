**Dagmar Ringe (1942-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The hallway outside the chemistry labs at MIT smells faintly of solvent and floor polish. Dagmar Ringe stands at the edge of a teaching laboratory she has run for years, glassware drying at the sinks, benches cleared for the next wave of undergraduates. She knows the routines by heart. What she does not have is a faculty line. Down the hall, her collaborator Gregory Petsko runs a research group with full institutional backing. They share ideas, grants, and students. Institutionally, they do not share status. By 1990, the imbalance has become impossible to ignore. The tension is not ambition but recognition. Ringe has already shaped how biochemists think about enzymes, yet she remains an "instructor," her research folded into the margins of someone else's lab. For nearly fifteen years she has worked part-time, raising two children, running teaching labs, and doing serious chemistry wherever space opens up. When Brandeis University offers her a tenured professorship and a joint appointment in chemistry and biochemistry, leaving MIT becomes a way of insisting, quietly but firmly, that her work stand on its own.

That insistence comes with intellectual restlessness. "Some work I've done is actually enzymology," Ringe has said, "but in order to stay there, I've had to learn many other disciplines. And not only that, to expand outward from simple enzymology, because the world is bigger than that and the problems are bigger than that." At Brandeis, she finally has room to act on that belief. In a shared lab moved wholesale from MIT, Ringe and Petsko push protein crystallography toward a view of enzymes not as static sculptures, but as moving machines.

One winter, the question becomes literal. Using ribonuclease A crystals at synchrotron facilities, Ringe and her colleagues cool enzymes through a wide temperature range. Below about 220 Kelvin, something strange happens: the protein still looks intact, but it can no longer bind its substrate. The atoms have stopped moving together. Biology, it turns out, requires warmth not just for chemistry, but for motion. Ringe and Petsko later call this a "glass transition," a physical limit on life at the molecular scale. The idea is simple. Enzymes are often imagined as rigid locks waiting for chemical keys. Ringe showed that they work because they flex and shift collectively. Cool them too much, and catalysis stops, even if the structure looks unchanged. By exploiting that cutoff, crystallographers can trap fleeting intermediates, turning reactions into something like frames in a movie.

The same sensibility runs through her later work. In her mid-fifties she solves the structure of alanine racemase, opening paths toward antibiotic design, since the enzyme makes an amino acid essential to bacterial cell walls but absent from human biochemistry. Later still, she pivots again, applying structural methods to neurodegenerative disease, arguing that Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and ALS are understood at the physiological level but not yet at the molecular one, and that the difficulties researchers now face are fundamentally molecular problems. Ringe's career never follows a straight line. Teaching labs, part-time appointments, reinvention. Yet she keeps returning to the same question: what must molecules be able to do, together, for life to work at all? In the lab, at last on her own terms, the answer keeps unfolding, one carefully cooled crystal at a time.
