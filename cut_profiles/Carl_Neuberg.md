**Carl Neuberg (1877-1956)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In a cramped laboratory at Brooklyn Polytechnic in 1950, Carl Neuberg bends over a fermentation vessel, his hands moving with the precision of habit. He is seventy-three years old, exiled, diminished in title, yet still commanding the chemistry of living cells with the authority of a man who has spent half a century decoding life's enzyme-catalyzed choreography. Decades earlier, he had been one of Europe's most influential biochemists, a man who defined the emerging field of biochemistry itself. Now, displaced by Nazi racial laws and stripped of his prestigious directorship at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute, he works in obscurity. Yet the pathways he discovered pulse through every living cell on Earth, a legacy no exile can fully erase. When Neuberg arrived in New York in the early 1940s, he was already beyond the age when most scientists expect to rebuild. He carried little more than his luggage. America was no paradise for displaced scholars; he felt reduced to an outsider. But his scientific impact was already deeply embedded in the foundations of modern biochemistry.

Born in 1877 to a Jewish merchant family in Hanover, Germany, Neuberg initially studied astronomy before switching to chemistry to satisfy his father's wishes. That pivot proved decisive. By the early twentieth century, Neuberg was working in Berlin at the forefront of a new discipline that sought to explain life through chemistry. In 1906, he helped institutionalize this emerging field by founding Biochemische Zeitschrift, one of the first journals devoted exclusively to biochemical research. As its editor for nearly thirty years, Neuberg shaped how biochemical knowledge was shared, debated, and standardized, helping unify a field that had previously been scattered across physiology and organic chemistry.

Neuberg's most influential scientific contribution came in 1911, when he identified an enzyme he called "carboxylase," now known as pyruvate decarboxylase. Studying yeast fermentation, Neuberg showed that pyruvate could be enzymatically converted into smaller molecules as part of alcoholic fermentation. This discovery provided crucial evidence that fermentation was not a mysterious "vital" process, but rather a sequence of discrete, enzyme-mediated steps. For modern students of biochemistry, this idea is foundational: metabolism proceeds through organized pathways, with enzymes passing intermediates along like runners in a relay race. Neuberg's work helped make that conceptual framework possible.

His theories were not merely academic. During World War I, Neuberg's understanding of fermentation was applied to industrial glycerol production, a compound essential for manufacturing explosives. The success of these processes reinforced the idea that biochemical principles could scale from laboratory benches to national industries.

Despite his prominence, Neuberg's career was abruptly derailed in the mid-1930s. As anti-Jewish policies spread through Germany, he was dismissed from his directorship at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute. After a difficult, multi-stage emigration through the Netherlands and Palestine, he reached the United States in 1940, as war engulfed Europe. There, he struggled to regain his former status, working in under-resourced laboratories at institutions like NYU and the Brooklyn Polytechnic Institute, dismissive of the modest academic titles he was offered there.

Neuberg never won a Nobel Prize, despite being nominated dozens of times in both Chemistry and Medicine. Yet his influence endures. He published nearly 900 papers, helped define biochemical terminology, and demonstrated, long before pathway diagrams filled textbooks, that life's chemistry unfolds one enzyme at a time. His story is not just one of discovery, but of resilience, displacement, and the enduring power of scientific ideas beyond the institutions that first sustain them.
