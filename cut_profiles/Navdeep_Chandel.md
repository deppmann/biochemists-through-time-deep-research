**Navdeep Chandel (1970-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Reactive oxygen species were the villains of cell biology, toxic byproducts of respiration that damaged DNA and accelerated aging. Navdeep Chandel argued they were also essential signals. The idea was contrarian enough that proving it required meticulous, patient work, the kind that rewards patience more than spectacle. Inside these cells, too small to see without magnification, lies a question that has lingered for decades: what, exactly, do mitochondria do?

For much of modern biology, the answer seemed settled. Mitochondria were the cell's power plants, efficient engines turning nutrients into energy. But as Chandel and his collaborators probe deeper, that certainty begins to fray. The tension is not dramatic but fundamental. If mitochondria do more than produce ATP, then entire assumptions about how cells sense and respond to their environment need rethinking.

That shift emerges from experiments linking mitochondria to signaling, specifically through reactive oxygen species, or ROS. Rather than treating ROS solely as toxic byproducts, Chandel's work shows them acting as messengers, helping cells interpret changes in oxygen availability. This reframing challenges a deeply ingrained narrative in biology. Chandel's lab has been at the forefront of shifting the field's view of mitochondria from mere "powerhouses" of the cell to signaling organelles in their own right, and the implications of that shift ripple outward.

The story turns as this signaling logic extends beyond oxygen sensing. In later work, Chandel's lab identifies the metabolite L-2-hydroxyglutarate, or L-2HG, as another molecular signal generated by mitochondria. Far from being inert metabolic debris, L-2HG influences how DNA and histones are chemically modified, shaping which genes turn on or off. In immune cells and stem cells, these changes help determine what a cell becomes and how it behaves.

At an introductory level, the idea is surprisingly intuitive. Mitochondria break down nutrients to keep cells alive, but in doing so they also generate small molecules that act like internal text messages. These messages reach the nucleus, where genes are regulated, and even affect epigenetic marks, the chemical tags that help lock cells into particular identities. Metabolism, in this view, is not backstage support. It is part of the decision-making machinery.

Chandel's research increasingly connects these insights to disease. In cancer biology, mitochondrial signaling can promote tumor growth under certain conditions. The same pathways that allow cells to adapt to low oxygen or stress can be exploited by tumors. This has drawn interest toward metabolic drugs such as metformin, though Chandel and others treat such applications carefully, aware of how easily mechanistic findings can be overstated when translated to therapy.

Throughout this work, a consistent thread runs through the lab's output: restraint. Claims are grounded in mechanism, and complexity is not smoothed away for convenience. The boundaries between helpful signaling and cellular damage remain an open question, especially when it comes to ROS. That uncertainty is not a flaw but a feature, an acknowledgment that living systems resist simple binaries.

Back at the bench, the mitochondria inside those cells continue their work, producing energy, releasing signals, shaping fate. The room offers no grand reveal, only incremental clarity. But within that quiet accumulation lies a reframed understanding of the cell, one where power generation and communication are inseparable, and where metabolism speaks.
