**David Rittenberg (1906-1970)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In the early twentieth century, biochemists believed the human body was mostly static. Food was burned for energy, waste was excreted, and the body's own tissues, muscle, fat, protein, were thought to remain largely unchanged unless starvation or disease intervened. David Rittenberg helped overturn this view, revealing instead that life is defined by constant molecular motion.

Born in 1906, Rittenberg was trained not as a biologist but as a physical chemist. At Columbia University, he studied under Harold Urey, who discovered deuterium (2H), a stable isotope of hydrogen. Rittenberg's doctoral work focused on how isotopes behave in chemical equilibria, knowledge that turned out to be perfectly suited for an emerging problem in biology: how to see metabolism in action.

At the time, metabolism was a black box. Biochemists could identify molecules entering and leaving the body, but they had no way to track individual atoms once they disappeared inside. Rittenberg realized that stable isotopes offered a solution. Because isotopes behave chemically like their common counterparts but can be detected analytically, they could serve as invisible tags.

When Rittenberg joined forces with Rudolph Schoenheimer, a refugee biochemist at Columbia's College of Physicians and Surgeons, the result was transformative. In 1935, the two scientists published a landmark paper showing that deuterium-labeled molecules could be followed through the animal body. What they found contradicted prevailing theory. Dietary fats were not simply burned for energy, they were incorporated into body fat, broken down, and rebuilt continuously. Proteins, once thought to be permanent structural components, were constantly synthesized and degraded. Even in a state of nutritional "balance," the body's molecules were in perpetual turnover.

These discoveries required extraordinary technical ingenuity. Commercial mass spectrometers did not yet exist, so Rittenberg helped build one from scratch to detect heavy nitrogen (15N), the key isotope for studying protein metabolism. Using this instrument, he showed that nitrogen from one amino acid could be redistributed throughout the body's entire amino acid pool. Proteins were not static scaffolds, they were dynamic participants in a vast metabolic network.

One of the most striking applications of this approach came from studies of red blood cells. By tracing 15N-labeled glycine into hemoglobin, Rittenberg and his collaborators, including David Shemin, were able to calculate the average lifespan of a human red blood cell, about 120 days. This was one of the first times that the lifespan of a human cell type had been measured directly in living people.

Rittenberg's work culminated in what became known as the "dynamic state" of body constituents: the idea that biological structure is maintained not by permanence, but by constant renewal. A muscle, a fat cell, or a red blood cell may appear stable, but its atoms are continuously replaced. The form persists, even though the material never does.

Today, this insight underlies modern biochemistry, from protein turnover to metabolomics and isotope tracing in cancer research. Long before high-throughput instruments and automated analysis, David Rittenberg showed that understanding life requires following atoms, not just molecules, and accepting that in biology, change is the rule, not the exception.
