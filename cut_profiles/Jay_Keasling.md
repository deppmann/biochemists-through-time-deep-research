**Jay Keasling (1964-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Jay Keasling stands before a glowing monitor in his Berkeley laboratory, surrounded by a team of fourteen researchers. The room is filled with the sterile, metallic scent of high-throughput sequencing and the rhythmic, low-frequency thrum of refrigerated incubators. It is 2006, and the multidisciplinary group is hunting for a genetic needle in a haystack. Their hands, once accustomed to the manual labor of glass pipettes, now navigate digital maps of the Artemisia annua plant genome. They are searching for the specific biological "software" that can turn a common microbe into a life-saving pharmaceutical factory. The stakes are measured in hundreds of thousands of lives, specifically, the children across Africa and Southeast Asia who die from malaria every year. While the sweet wormwood plant produces artemisinin, the world's most effective antimalarial, the natural supply is trapped in a chaotic cycle of harvest fluctuations and volatile prices. Keasling's team has already achieved a minor miracle by engineering yeast to produce amorphadiene, a hydrocarbon precursor. But amorphadiene is biologically inert.

Without identifying the specific plant gene that triggers the three-step oxidation into artemisinic acid, the $53 million investment from the Gates Foundation will fail to deliver a usable drug. The breakthrough arrives with the identification of a novel cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, CYP71AV1. This gene is the critical bridge, the enzymatic worker capable of performing the complex chemistry that previously only existed in the leaves of a shrub. For Keasling, this discovery validates a philosophy that treats biology as a branch of mechanical engineering. As he explained in a 2006 interview, "If you understand these controls, you can make the cell do something that it wouldn't necessarily want to do... That's really where I started thinking about this integration of engineering and biology, not to build tanks to grow microbes but actually to go in and reengineer the cell as a chemical reactor." This reengineering includes sophisticated "metabolic plumbing" of the isoprenoid pathway, the same biochemical route cells use to synthesize cholesterol.

To maximize production, the team inserts a truncated, soluble form of the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase, effectively bypassing the cell's natural feedback inhibition, a biological thermostat that usually shuts the system down. This bypass allows the yeast to accumulate massive quantities of farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP), a universal building block. When the newly discovered CYP71AV1 enzyme is plumbed into this system along with its redox partners, the yeast begins to churn out artemisinic acid at concentrations high enough for industrial scale-up. The identification of this single gene eventually enabled industrial vats in Italy to produce enough semi-synthetic medicine for 150 million treatments annually. For Keasling, the technical triumph is an echo of a harder life on a Nebraska farm, where he spent his youth managing swine and cattle under the rule that if something broke, you fixed it yourself. The smell of manure and dirt from his high school years has been replaced by the odorless precision of synthetic code, but the heuristic of the "sole proprietorship" farm remains.

He has moved biology past the "vacuum tube" stage and into the era of the biological Pentium chip, treating the cell as a productive worker that can be repaired and optimized for the global good.
