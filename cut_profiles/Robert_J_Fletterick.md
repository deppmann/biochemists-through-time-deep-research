**Robert J. Fletterick (1941-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In December 1996, thousands of cell biologists sat quietly in a convention hall as an unfamiliar protein structure appeared on the screen. It was kinesin, a molecular motor that carries cargo along microtubules inside cells. When the atomic model rotated into view, the surprise became unmistakable: kinesin's motor domain looked nearly identical to myosin, the protein responsible for muscle contraction. The two proteins had no obvious sequence similarity and worked on different cellular tracks, yet they shared the same mechanical core. For Robert J. Fletterick, whose laboratory had solved the structure, this moment captured the power of structural biology, to reveal deep biological truths that could not be guessed from sequences alone.

That power had shaped Fletterick's career from the beginning. In 1970, as a postdoctoral fellow at Yale University, he made a risky but decisive move: he left an established laboratory to join the newly formed group of a young assistant professor, Thomas Steitz. Together, working in a shared X-ray crystallography core facility, they tackled yeast hexokinase, an enzyme central to glucose metabolism. By solving structures of the enzyme both with and without its substrate, they produced the first direct visual proof of Daniel Koshland's "induced fit" hypothesis. The enzyme's two lobes closed dramatically around glucose, showing that proteins are not rigid tools but dynamic machines. These images became some of the most reproduced figures in biochemistry textbooks and permanently changed how enzymes are taught to students.

Fletterick continued to pursue the question of how protein shape controls function when he turned to glycogen phosphorylase, the enzyme that initiates glycogen breakdown. In the mid-1970s, his group at the University of Alberta raced against Louise Johnson's team at Oxford to solve the structure of this massive, regulated enzyme. The competition culminated in simultaneous presentations at a 1976 crystallography meeting, where both groups revealed unusually similar protein folds despite working on different crystal forms. A later correction, when Johnson's data showed that Fletterick's group had misplaced a key cofactor, highlighted something equally important: the self-correcting, collaborative nature of science. The final structures revealed that regulatory signals act tens of angstroms away from the catalytic site, providing a physical explanation for allostery that remains central to introductory biochemistry.

At UCSF, where Fletterick spent most of his career, his work expanded from metabolic enzymes to molecular motors and nuclear receptors. The kinesin structure confirmed that life uses a conserved mechanical design to convert chemical energy into motion. Later, his lab uncovered a hidden regulatory site, called BF-3, on the androgen receptor, a protein central to prostate cancer. This "back door" site offered a new strategy for drug design, showing how structural biology could expose vulnerabilities that traditional approaches had missed.

Despite the technical intensity of his work, Fletterick is remembered by students and colleagues for his kindness and mentorship. Known simply as "Flett," he was famous for long office conversations about science, skiing, and life. Even as a senior scientist, he emphasized balance, limiting work hours to spend time with his family and remaining active outdoors. Reflecting on his career, he liked to say that the work of his lab kept uncovering the same lesson in different guises: that structure is function and function is structure. For generations of biochemistry students, those designs continue to define how proteins are understood, not as static diagrams, but as moving, regulated machines.
