**Mitchell Lazar (1956-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Mitchell Lazar sits in a dimly lit lecture hall at MIT, listening to a professor describe how individual neurotransmitters shape the architecture of thought and emotion. It is the late 1970s. He had come to chemistry expecting equations, reaction mechanisms, crystalline structures. Instead he hears that serotonin, dopamine, and acetylcholine are not abstract chemicals but the molecular basis of human experience. The lecture lands with the force of recognition. What he wants, it turns out, is to understand the biochemistry of the mind, the way molecules become behavior. He pivots, and takes the MD-PhD path at Stanford, marking himself as someone unwilling to choose between healing and understanding.

Years later, in 2001, Lazar's team at the University of Pennsylvania is screening fat cells to work out how a class of diabetes drugs called thiazolidinediones does what it does. The drugs improve insulin sensitivity; the mechanism is hidden. The team reasons backward. If a drug improves insulin sensitivity, something must be changing at the level of gene expression. They screen thousands of genes and one stands out: treat the fat cells with the drug, and this gene goes quiet. They isolate what it produces and give it a name. Resistin.

The discovery is technically straightforward and conceptually radical. Fat tissue, long dismissed as a passive storage depot, is an endocrine organ, an active manufacturer of hormones that talk to the rest of the body. Resistin ties obesity to type 2 diabetes. But Lazar resists the simplification, including his own. Later work shows that in humans, resistin comes not mainly from fat cells but from immune cells, the macrophages. The story becomes more intricate, more contingent, less like a magic bullet and more like a biological conversation.

Ask him for the perfect diet and he will disappoint you. The data, he says, are not there yet, and he is openly skeptical of anyone who dresses up a confident instruction about when and what to eat in scientific language it has not earned. He believes rigorous science will eventually win out over speculation; he refuses to pretend that certainty has arrived before it has. "Our destinies are a combination of our genes and our environments," he says. The sentence holds humility and ambition at once, conceding what he does not know while insisting the question is worth the trouble.

The biochemistry at the center of his work runs on principles that look simple and govern complexity. Nuclear receptors are proteins that bind DNA and switch genes on or off. Lazar found that one of them, Rev-erbα, acts as a metabolic clock tied to the body's circadian rhythm: when it lands on the DNA of certain genes at certain hours, it recruits corepressor proteins, NCoR and HDAC3, that silence them, keeping metabolism synchronized with light and dark. The receptor's own ligand turns out to be heme, the same iron-bearing group that carries oxygen in hemoglobin. Metabolism, oxygen, and time meet in one pocket of one protein.

He has been elected to the National Academy of Medicine, the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, and the National Academy of Sciences. What defines him is not the membership but the intellectual honesty: he says publicly when he does not know. In a 2021 presidential address to the Association of American Physicians, published in the Journal of Clinical Investigation as "Novel biomedical research must not be a work of fiction," he warned that unconscious bias and pressure to tell a clean story push researchers toward cherry-picked results, and argued that science ought to reward reproducibility itself, not just novelty. The commitment traces back to that lecture hall at MIT, where he learned that molecules and minds are inseparable, and that understanding how requires patience, rigor, and a refusal to settle for less than the truth.
