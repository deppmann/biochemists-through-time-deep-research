**Irwin Kuntz (1935-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The Department of Pharmaceutical Chemistry at UCSF in the early 1980s was a cramped landscape of early computational workstations and humming hardware. There, Irwin "Tack" Kuntz, working with Renee DesJarlais and Robert Sheridan, set out to do something that in 1982 felt like digital alchemy. They were not looking for atoms; they were looking for the voids where atoms might go, generating clusters of tiny, overlapping spheres, a negative image, to map the jagged interior of a protein's binding pocket. For decades, finding a new drug had largely meant testing thousands of chemicals against a disease and hoping for a hit. Kuntz bet that the search could instead be automated through geometry: if the shape of a binding site could be described precisely enough, a computer might sift through a library of candidate molecules and find ones that fit, without a single test tube being touched.

That bet became DOCK, the algorithm his group published in 1982 in the Journal of Molecular Biology. In introductory biochemistry, students learn that enzymes and substrates fit together like a hand in a glove. Kuntz's program translated that metaphor into a computable thermodynamic problem, seeking the binding pose with the most favorable free energy of interaction. By matching the geometry of a small-molecule ligand to the sphere centers describing a protein's active site, the software estimated which orientations would pack most stably, turning the fuzzy idea of molecular "fit" into a calculation of steric attraction and repulsion. Tested first on known structures like the heme-myoglobin complex, the method reproduced experimental binding geometries and, over the following decades, grew into one of the field's standard tools for structure-based drug design.

The lab Kuntz built proved as durable as his code. When the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake shook the Parnassus campus, the group kept working, continuing to refine the custom scripts they used for data processing and debating how to extend the method from rigid shapes to flexible, shifting molecules. What began as a geometric abstraction in a crowded San Francisco lab eventually became a widely used standard in computational chemistry, helping move parts of early-stage drug discovery from trial and error toward a more rational, structure-guided search.
