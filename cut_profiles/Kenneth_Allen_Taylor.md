**Kenneth Allen Taylor (1946-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Liquid nitrogen fogs the air in the Donner Laboratory at UC Berkeley. Kenneth Allen Taylor steadies his hands over a stainless-steel drum he machined himself, its surface rimed with cold. The JEM 100B electron microscope hums behind him, a machine never meant to accept a specimen soaked in water and plunged into vacuum. He works quickly. Any pause invites frost, ambient moisture crystallizing onto the frozen grid, erasing the very structure he is trying to see. The risk is not subtle. In 1974, the assumption is near-universal: hydrated protein crystals cannot survive freezing in liquid nitrogen without shattering their internal order. Ice, everyone agrees, will pulverize biology. Taylor, working as a doctoral student under Robert Glaeser, is betting that the dogma is wrong, and that careful engineering can buy a few seconds of protection. "Our goal was simply to develop a method that would preserve crystalline order in protein crystals within the EM," he later explains, recalling how "it was widely assumed that a protein crystal embedded in an aqueous solvent could not be frozen in liquid nitrogen and still retain its crystallinity." The diffraction pattern that appears, spots extending to 3.4 angstroms, does not arrive as a cinematic surprise. It is the product of iterative tinkering: a rotating airlock, a frost protector filled with cold nitrogen gas, procedures rehearsed until they work. Yet the implication is seismic. Frozen, hydrated protein crystals can retain atomic order in a vacuum. Electrons, not just X-rays, can reach toward the scale of atoms. That pursuit begins earlier, in a lecture hall at North Carolina State University. In 1971, Taylor listens as Richard Dickerson describes the atomic structure of cytochrome c. The image of a protein rendered atom by atom redirects him. Trained in textile chemistry, he has learned to think in fibers and polymers; now he wants resolution, the kind that reveals how molecular machines are built. He carries that benchmark with him to Berkeley, then across the Atlantic in 1976 to the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, where Hugh Huxley's group is trying to understand how muscle generates force. Muscle proves far less accommodating than catalase crystals. Its filaments are long, helical, restless. Taylor shifts from flat images to early three-dimensional reconstructions, trying to catch myosin heads mid-action. The work demands patience and a tolerance for skepticism, especially when raw images look nearly blank. The information, he learns, is there, but it must be coaxed out by averaging and geometry. By 1999, at Florida State University, that patience yields a decisive image. Working closely with his wife and collaborator Dianne Taylor, he visualizes the inhibited state of smooth muscle myosin. Two heads from the same molecule fold against each other, one physically blocking the other's ability to bind actin. It is a mechanical off-switch: a way for muscle to conserve energy when relaxed. The idea is simple to picture. Muscle contraction depends on myosin heads grabbing actin and pulling, fueled by ATP. Taylor's structures show how, in the resting state, that grab is prevented, not by chemistry alone, but by shape. Like a safety latch, the heads lock themselves until a signal releases them. Half a century after the Dickerson lecture, the siege on resolution closes its arc. In Tallahassee, using a Titan Krios microscope and AI-assisted models, Taylor's group resolves insect flight muscle thick filaments at 2.7 angstroms. The myosin tail, an immense, coiled polymer, appears atom by atom, woven into its native lattice. The image does not end the story so much as echo its beginning: cold metal, careful engineering, and a belief that structure, if preserved, will eventually give itself up.
