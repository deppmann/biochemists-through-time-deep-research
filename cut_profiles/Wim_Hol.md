**Wim Hol (1943-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Through protein crystallography applied to pathogenic microorganisms, Hol demonstrated that fundamental protein structural principles -- such as the alpha-helix macro-dipole and the 'Nest' anion-binding site -- govern biological function and enabled the development of structural genomics approaches targeting neglected tropical diseases. His work established that detailed structural understanding of disease-causing proteins provides both mechanistic insight into pathogenesis and a foundation for rational drug design.

In a lecture room at the University of Eindhoven, Wim G. J. Hol sits in a formal guest lecture, leaning forward as the speaker moves through slides. The projector ticks to the next image; the room stills. Hol raises his hand and asks the question that doesn't fit the decorum: why study the structure of a plant protein like papain when there are "so many pressing medical problems to solve"? The tension is instant. A skeptical undergraduate can sound reckless, or self-important, and this is a public challenge to an established scientist. But Hol is also naming an ethical pressure that will shadow him: if you spend years mapping molecules, does anyone sick ever feel the difference? Jan Drenth answers without humiliation. In the account preserved later, his response is "patient and insightful," strong enough to redirect Hol's trajectory and begin a decades-long relationship between mentor and mentee. Hol follows Drenth into crystallography at Groningen and adopts the older scientist's premise, that fundamentals can be the only honest route to medicine. That premise shapes Hol's lab culture as much as his experiments. Looking back on what his group produced, he refuses the lone-genius story: "All these results could only be obtained because of. the fabulous members of my group over the years. talented, enthusiastic and dedicated young scientists joining efforts to understand the marvelous action of proteins." The sentence carries a quiet demand: the work matters, so the people doing it matter. His science keeps returning to invisible forces made legible. At Groningen, Hol helps show that the small polarities of individual peptide bonds line up inside an α-helix to create a macro-dipole, strong enough to "steer" molecules toward active sites. Later, his group identifies another subtle shape, the "Nest," a tiny backbone depression that often sits where a protein needs to hold anions like phosphate or sulfate steady. For non-specialists, the bridge is simple: a peptide bond is slightly polar, like a weak magnet. Put many of them into the repeating spiral of an α-helix, and the pulls add up into a directional tug that can attract negatively charged groups such as phosphates into the right pocket. Pair that tug with a "Nest" that cradles an ion, and protein "structure" starts behaving like a set of physical tools. In Seattle, the old Eindhoven question becomes an operating system. Hol helps launch the Structural Genomics of Pathogenic Protozoa (SGPP), a consortium built to solve structures from neglected pathogens at scale. It is practical, almost stubbornly so: expression tricks like the "RIG" plasmid to overcome malaria codon bias, fragment "cocktails" for finding chemical footholds, and rapid data sharing so other labs can build on the coordinates. Hol's reasons are plain. He chooses diseases like sleeping sickness and malaria because they "preferentially affect poor nations" and get ignored when profit is the gatekeeper. The mentor lens comes full circle here: he is no longer the student in the lecture hall, but the person building rooms, labs, pipelines, a culture, where younger scientists can ask the same uncomfortable question and then do something with it.
