**Alton Meister (1922-1995)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

At Cornell University Medical College, Alton Meister's Friday group meetings were known as a benevolent inquisition. He would study the data in silence, then point to the single outlier everyone else had dismissed, a gel band slightly off, a rate constant that didn't fit, and insist it was not error but evidence. That instinct for anomaly drove a career spent mapping the biochemistry of glutathione, a molecule most of his contemporaries considered too simple to be interesting.

Meister's precision was forged early. After earning his bachelor's degree from Harvard in 1942 and his MD from Cornell University Medical College in 1945, an accelerated wartime pace that put him at the bedside of patients in his early twenties, he found himself diagnosing metabolic failures he could not yet fully explain. He grew weary of clinical medicine's messiness, gravitating toward the National Cancer Institute where chemical elegance offered clarity. By 33, he was a full professor and chairman of biochemistry at Tufts, viewing the cell as a masterpiece of architectural engineering.

His greatest discovery was the gamma-glutamyl cycle, often called the "Meister Cycle." Before Meister, glutathione was dismissed as a simple redox buffer. Meister revealed something more active: a coordinated loop of six enzymes that use ATP energy to transport amino acids across cell membranes. Glutathione acts as the carrier, capturing amino acids and delivering them into the cytoplasm, then being meticulously reconstructed for the next cycle. It exemplifies metabolic efficiency, the cell spends energy moving building blocks, then rebuilds its tools.

Meister's laboratory at Cornell was legendary for rigor. He carried a pocket notebook to capture enzyme mechanism insights, an instinct for anomaly that eventually led to Buthionine Sulfoximine (BSO), a specific inhibitor of glutathione synthesis that became critical in oncology, making cancer cells vulnerable to chemotherapy.

Alton Meister's life was defined by refusal to accept chaos. Whether pipetting at a bench or editing Advances in Enzymology, the journal he oversaw for more than two decades, he sought the same thing: coherent, circular logic, in a field growing almost too fast for any one person to fully master.

The founders built biochemistry's vocabulary, atoms rearranged, energy tracked, pathways mapped. What came next was the question those tools were built to answer: how does a cell read its own instructions?
