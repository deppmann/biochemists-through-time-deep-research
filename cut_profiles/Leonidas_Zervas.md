**Leonidas Zervas (1902-1980)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Leonidas Zervas stands in the chemistry building in Athens after the occupation, the laboratory no longer usable. The benches are there, but the work is not. Equipment is gone. Research has stopped. Dust settles on glassware that once mattered. What remains is the decision of what comes next: whether science can be restarted at all, and if so, how. The tension is not abstract. Zervas has been imprisoned during the war and involved in the Greek Resistance; his laboratory has been destroyed. The international networks that once carried reagents, letters, and ideas across borders have collapsed. Peptide chemistry, the careful, incremental assembly of molecules, depends on stability and continuity, and Zervas has neither. Rebuilding is not a matter of inspiration but of logistics, permission, and persistence, pursued in a country still struggling to steady itself. That persistence is visible years earlier, in the choice he makes in 1934. The Nazi state has just pushed Max Bergmann out of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for being Jewish, and Bergmann is rebuilding his laboratory an ocean away, at the Rockefeller Institute in New York. Zervas, by then a close friend and the institute's vice-director in Dresden, chooses to follow him rather than stay behind in a Germany that has just shown what it does to Jewish scientists and their collaborators. It costs him the position he has spent nearly a decade building. The choice reveals how he thinks: loyalty to a working partnership and a readiness to start over the moment the ground shifts. It was in that same Dresden laboratory, years before the exile, that Zervas and Bergmann had already solved a stubborn chemical problem: how to build peptides step by step without reactions spiraling out of control. Amino acids are chemically eager; left unrestrained, they react in unwanted ways. Zervas and Bergmann introduced a removable "cap" for the amino group, the carboxybenzyl, or Z, protecting group, that could be put on, taken off, and put back on as needed. It allowed chemists to assemble peptides deliberately, one bond at a time, instead of hoping for order from chemical chaos. After the war, the problem Zervas faces is no longer molecular but institutional. He returns to Athens and begins again, rebuilding laboratories, training students, and reconnecting Greek chemistry to the wider scientific world. The work is slower now, shaped by shortages and politics, but it continues. He helps secure resources, organizes research groups, and restores a sense of forward motion where it had stalled. Students who pass through his laboratory go on to tackle ambitious targets, including complex hormone syntheses that once seemed unreachable. The story circles back to reconstruction. The same habits visible in his 1934 choice to follow Bergmann across an ocean, attention to process, respect for collaboration, and forward motion despite uncertainty, govern his postwar life. Zervas does not erase the loss of his laboratory; he works around it. What survives is not a single experiment or method, but a way of proceeding: finish what you can, ask for guidance when needed, and start the research journey again as soon as the path reopens, even when the room is still half empty.
