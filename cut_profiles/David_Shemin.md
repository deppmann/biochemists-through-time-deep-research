**David Shemin (1911-1991)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In 1945, within the quiet halls of Columbia University, David Shemin prepared a meal that would have horrified a modern safety board. On his desk sat a glass containing sixty-six grams of glycine. Shemin had spent months of painstaking labor and a significant portion of his research budget labeling glycine with 15N, a heavy, stable isotope of nitrogen. In an era before automated sequencers, Shemin realized that the only way to solve the mystery of where our blood comes from was to turn his own body into a living laboratory.

For decades, the scientific community had debated the origin of heme, the deep red, oxygen carrying core of hemoglobin. Was it built from complex proteins? Was it a byproduct of recycled cellular waste? Shemin suspected a humbler origin in the simple amino acid glycine. Tracking an atom's journey through the human body in the 1940s was like trying to follow a single raindrop through a hurricane. To catch the "heavy" nitrogen in the act of becoming blood, he needed a massive starting dose. Over the course of three days, Shemin swallowed the isotope laden mixture and waited.

The real work began after the feast. For the next several weeks, Shemin became his own most frequent patient. He drew his own blood at precise intervals, often in the early morning or late at night. As he processed his own samples through a mass spectrometer, a beautiful curve began to emerge on his graph paper. The 15N was not just passing through his system. It was being woven directly into his new red blood cells, watching his body build itself in real time.

The "Eureka" moment arrived with a secondary discovery that was even more dramatic than the first. As Shemin plotted the isotope concentration over one hundred days, he expected the levels to decline gradually as the glycine was metabolized. Instead, the concentration stayed strikingly steady for nearly four months. Then, it plummeted with sudden, mathematical precision. In that moment, Shemin realized he had not just found the precursor to heme. He had discovered the lifespan of the human red blood cell. They were not dying randomly. They were programmed to live for approximately one hundred and twenty-seven days.

Shemin's own writing on the porphyrin work returned again and again to the same theme: the basic chemical reactions of the cell are simple, and there is a deep biochemical unity in living matter. This daring self-experiment proved his point: the body is not a static machine, but a dynamic, ever-flowing river of molecules. Shemin's willingness to "eat the science" bridged the gap between the Citric Acid Cycle and the very blood that allowed him to breathe. It remains one of the most decisive examples of scientific bravado. It was a moment when a researcher looked at a flask of heavy nitrogen and decided that the best way to understand life was to literally consume the question. By the time the last of the heavy nitrogen left his system, Shemin had not only mapped the chemical architecture of blood but had also proven that even the most complex biological mysteries could be solved with a little bit of isotope and a lot of guts.
