**Kasimir Fajans (1887-1975)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The photograph holds still, but the workroom feels busy. Kasimir Fajans stands beside Bror Holmberg, the pair posed in front of shelves packed with glassware, bottles, flasks, and stoppered vessels lined up like a private alphabet of chemistry (Science History Institute). Behind them, lab order becomes a kind of argument: this is a place built to separate, to name, to sort. That urge, to impose a clean scheme on a messy world, will make Fajans famous and then, late in life, leave him stranded. Even when he writes about radioactivity at its most unruly, he reaches for structure. "The starting point for these considerations," he insists in a 1913 paper, "is the connection that I have established between the type of a radioactive transformation and the electrochemical character of the radio element being considered" (ChemTeam). It's not a flourish. It's a declaration of method: the chaos will yield, if you classify it correctly. He has reasons to believe it. A few years earlier, he spends 1910--1911 in Ernest Rutherford's Manchester laboratory, absorbing a style of research that treats radioactive change as something you can track, step by step, through evidence rather than wonder (Kragh). By 1913, he is putting the logic on the page. In the same translated excerpt, he emphasizes that radioactive transformations fall into two classes, alpha and beta, each pushing an element through predictable shifts in periodic identity (ChemTeam). The claim is bold not because it is theatrical, but because it is usable: a working map for other chemists. And then comes the kind of episode that makes maps controversial. That year, Fajans and his assistant Oswald Helmuth Göhring (sometimes called Otto Göhring in the historical literature) identify a short-lived isotope associated with element 91 and propose the name brevium, a nod to its brief existence (VanderKrogt; Egdell). Later retellings can smooth this into a single heroic line, discovery, christening, permanence, but the element's history is knottier, bound up with isotopes and later confirmation that other researchers are also credited with (RSC; Egdell). Even his neatest labels have to fight for stable ground. Here is the basic idea, without the mystique. Radioactive atoms can transform by ejecting particles. An alpha emission throws off a helium nucleus; a beta emission releases an electron (ChemTeam). Those changes alter the atom's identity, what "element" it is, so decay can be followed like a trail of footprints. Once scientists could identify and handle isotopes with confidence, the same tracking instinct powered biochemical tracer work: label atoms, watch where they go, and infer the hidden steps of reactions (Chemistry World). Fajans's own life becomes a kind of displacement story. He directs a laboratory for physical chemistry in Munich from 1932 to 1935, then leaves Germany as conditions "became serious," arriving at the University of Michigan in the mid-1930s, sources differ on whether the move is 1935 or 1936 (College of LSA; Science History Institute). In Michigan, he is remembered as a brilliant guide for younger researchers, someone who could inspire and steer work without dulling it (College of LSA). The image of the orderly shelves survives: a man still trying to make knowledge stand in rows. But controversy returns in his final decades. Fajans promotes a "quanticule" theory of chemical bonding and finds a receptive audience in industry, seminars at Shell in 1953; visits to 3M in 1952 and 1956, and a week of talks in October 1959 (Bulletin for the History of Chemistry). The academic world, the same historians report, remains largely hostile, with one commentator dismissing the theory as a great scientist's lapse (Bulletin for the History of Chemistry). The laboratory shelves, once a symbol of control, start to look like a warning: classification can illuminate a field, or trap a person inside his own system.
