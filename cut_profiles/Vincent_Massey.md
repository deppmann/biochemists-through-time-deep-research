**Vincent Massey (1926-2002)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

It is 9:00 PM in an Ann Arbor garden, and Vincent Massey is standing among a cadre of friends, watching a rare, heavy-scented miracle. The massive, white petals of the night-blooming cereus are unfurling, a spectacle that occurs only once a year and lasts mere hours. Massey doesn't just watch; he drinks bottles of wine with his guests, his eyes twinkling as he observes the transient beauty of a flower that will be gone by dawn. This obsession with the fleeting is more than a hobby; it is the fundamental rhythm of his life. This fascination defines the central tension of his scientific existence. In the mid-20th century, the chemical reactions sustaining life are largely understood as static snapshots, the "before" and "after" of metabolic pathways. The critical transitions where energy actually transforms, the millisecond-long intermediates, remain invisible to standard laboratory tools. For Massey, the stakes are the very mechanics of how vitamins drive the cellular engine; to miss the intermediate is to miss the essence of the work. Massey's journey to these invisible flickers began far from the elite labs of Michigan. Born into a family of fishermen in the coastal Australian village of Berkeley, he spent his youth gutting the day's catch. To reach the "world of ideas" at his high school, he braved a four-hour daily journey, cycling five miles to a train station, riding the rails, and walking the final leg. When asked if he ever considered following his father's manual trade, Massey's response was immediate: "Not on your life!" He funded his first experiments by selling wild mushrooms collected after heavy rains, eventually trading the sea for the sting of sulfuric acid in a makeshift bedroom lab. He found his calling in the study of flavins, versatile molecules derived from vitamin B2 that manage the cell's electron traffic. Unlike many cofactors that transfer electrons in pairs, the isoalloxazine ring of a flavin can handle them one at a time, serving as a critical bridge between substrates and the mitochondrial transport chain. To capture these interactions, Massey popularized stopped-flow spectrophotometry, a technique that rapidly fires enzyme and substrate into a chamber to monitor absorbance changes within milliseconds. This allowed his team to catch the C(4a)-hydroperoxyflavin, a high-energy species that activates oxygen for hydroxylation. His lab even famously discovered that the "Old Yellow Enzyme" was actually green when active, the result of a fleeting charge-transfer complex that had eluded researchers for decades. In his later years, Massey's vitality remained "all flow without stop," even as he turned his fierce independence toward caring for his wife after a stroke. He continued to champion pure, fundamental research, dismissing the pressure to follow lucrative scientific trends with a sharp, "So what!" At his memorial, the somber notes of the Goldberg Variations gave way to a cheery Grainger folk tune as the audience departed. It was a final nod to a man who understood that while the most essential moments are often the most transient, they are the only ones worth capturing.
