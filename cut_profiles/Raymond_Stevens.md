**Raymond Stevens (1963-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In a lab split between Stanford and La Jolla, Raymond Stevens pursued a molecular target that had embarrassed structural biologists for decades: the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR). These seven-transmembrane proteins relay signals from hormones, neurotransmitters, and odorants into cells. They are the largest family of drug targets in the human body. Yet solving their structure seemed impossible. GPCRs are inherently flexible, sampling multiple conformational states. They are deeply embedded in the lipid bilayer, resisting crystallization. Every attempt to crystallize them produced microcrystals, disordered diffraction patterns, or nothing at all.

The breakthrough came not from better crystals but from better methodology. Stevens and colleagues, including Brian Kobilka, discovered that embedding GPCRs in a cubic lipid membrane mimicking the native bilayer stabilized them. The lipidic cubic phase preserved the protein's flexibility while providing ordered lattice contacts sufficient for diffraction. In 2007, the structure of the human β2-adrenergic receptor resolved at atomic resolution, revealing the seven helices transmembrane architecture.

The biochemical mechanism of GPCR signaling became visible in atomic detail. When a ligand (adrenaline, in the case of the β2-adrenergic receptor) binds to the extracellular portion of the GPCR, it induces a conformational change. The intracellular loops shift, creating a new surface. This new surface recruits the heterotrimeric G protein (composed of Gα, Gβ, and Gγ subunits). The interaction triggers nucleotide exchange: GDP bound to Gα is released, and GTP (more abundant in the cytosol) binds. GTP-bound Gα dissociates from Gβγ and activates downstream effectors, adenylyl cyclase, producing cAMP; phospholipase C, generating IP3 and DAG; or ion channels.

The mechanism depends on precise molecular choreography. Ligand binding is not a simple lock-and-key interaction but an induced-fit process where both receptor and ligand conformationally adapt. Kobilka's later work with cryo-EM revealed that GPCRs actually sample multiple conformations even when bound to agonist ligands. The activated conformation is stabilized, but others remain accessible. This ensemble view explained how the same receptor could activate different G proteins or engage β-arrestin signaling, the activated conformation selected depends on the ligand identity and cellular context.

Stevens continued solving GPCR structures: the κ-opioid receptor (2012), revealing pain and addiction signaling; the chemokine receptor CCR5, central to HIV cell entry; and Smoothened, a hedgehog pathway receptor distinct from classical GPCRs in its unusual ligand binding mode (cholesterol, not a soluble small molecule). Each structure challenged existing classification systems and revealed new mechanistic principles. Stevens questioned the "magic number 7" classification, why assume all seven-transmembrane receptors share a unified mechanism?

In 2009, Stevens authored a retraction of an earlier structural paper. The decision to publicly correct erroneous data, attaching his name to failure, reflected his scientific ethic: the map must be honest. Structural biology's utility depends on accuracy. A beautiful but incorrect structure is worse than no structure at all.

This same rigor drove his commercial ventures. Receptos, the company Stevens co-founded, was built on the principle that precise understanding of receptor structure enables rational drug design. Its lead program, an oral modulator of the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor S1P1, grew directly out of the structure the company had solved for that receptor, and it became ozanimod, an approved treatment for multiple sclerosis and ulcerative colitis. When Celgene acquired Receptos in 2015, Stevens considered what remained: advancing the structural biology platform itself, not just harvesting single-target structures. He continued driving innovation in membrane protein crystallography and cryo-EM methodology, recognizing that a solved structure is not the end of science but the beginning of mechanism understanding.

Back in the lab, Stevens maintains the discipline of crystal optimization, the patient, methodical work of screening conditions, refining techniques, and pushing crystallography's boundaries. Each new structure is a ridge discovered on an unexplored mountain. The pattern repeats: identify a target others avoid, build the tools to visualize it, let the structure guide the next questions. For Stevens, that iterative process, failure, methodological innovation, structural revelation, is the core of structural biochemistry.
