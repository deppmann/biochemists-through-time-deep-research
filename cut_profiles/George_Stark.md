**George Stark (1933-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

The air inside the Columbia University cold room is heavy with humidity and the sharp, vegetal scent of raw squash. George Stark and a labmate hunch over bushels of yellow crook-necked vegetables, their fingers numbing as they rhythmically peel away the outer skin. To stave off the monotony of the task, they fill the refrigerated space with raucous humor. It is the late 1950s, and this grueling, tactile labor is part of a quest to isolate an enzyme called ascorbate oxidase. For Stark, these hours in the cold are a relief compared to the "Stone Age" of his early medical studies. He had abandoned a path in medicine after finding the rote memorization of anatomy, naming every nerve in a frog, entirely impossible. He required a different way to organize the world. "I can remember things very well if I can link them in a logical chain," Stark later reflected, "but the names of all these nerves and so forth in the frog were not linkable in a logical chain for me!" Biochemistry offered that chain, replacing descriptive chaos with the coherent, predictable architecture of chemical bonds. This architectural mind led Stark to solve a persistent puzzle at Rockefeller University: why did proteins lose their function when placed in urea? While others focused on the protein's shape, Stark examined the solution's chemistry. He discovered that urea slowly breaks down into cyanate, which then attacks and permanently alters the amino acids of the protein, a process called carbamylation. By identifying this hidden reaction, he transformed a frustrating laboratory mystery into a logical sequence of molecular events. Stark's greatest gift to the field was building the frameworks that allowed other scientists to see the cell's internal logic. In the mid-1970s, detecting RNA meant freezing a gel slab and hacking it into 100 tiny pieces with a device resembling a kitchen egg slicer. Frustrated by this "ridiculously cumbersome" method, Stark's group developed a way to transfer those RNA molecules onto specially treated paper where they could be easily identified with radioactive probes. They called it the Northern blot, a pun on the Southern blot used for DNA, and it allowed researchers to quantify gene expression in living tissue. His pursuit of logical structures eventually took him into the heart of the cell's communication lines. By creating mutant human cell lines that couldn't respond to interferon, Stark's lab identified Tyk2, the first of the Janus kinases (JAK). This discovery established the JAK-STAT orthodoxy, explaining how a signal at the cell surface travels directly to the nucleus to trigger a response. Decades later, Stark remains at the bench, still applying the same mechanistic rigor to old molecules. He recently revisited PALA, a potent inhibitor he synthesized in the 1970s. PALA acts as a transition state analog; it is a stable molecule that "mimics" the high-energy, fleeting shape an enzyme's target takes during a reaction. While it was too toxic as a high-dose cancer drug, Stark discovered that at low doses, it acts as an immune stimulant. It is a full circle for a scientist who has spent his life trading the "Stone Age" of memorization for the enduring logic of the machine.
