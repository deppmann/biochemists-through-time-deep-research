**Stephen Sprang (1952-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Using X-ray crystallography, Sprang solved the first structures of heterotrimeric G proteins bound to effector enzymes, revealing the conformational changes and catalytic mechanisms by which hormone signals are transduced across cell membranes. His structures demonstrated how a small rotation of protein domains converts an inactive GDP-bound state into an active GTP-bound form, establishing the molecular basis for signal amplification in cellular communication.

In a lab at UT Southwestern in Dallas, a tray of protein crystals sits at the center of an experiment designed to catch motion without letting it escape. David Coleman soaks Gα·GDP crystals in increasing magnesium, step by step, while the team hunts for structures at about 2 Å resolution that can pin down a signal switch mid-transition. In the data that come back, a tetrahedral AlF₄⁻ ion appears in the active site, standing in for the γ-phosphate of GTP, and a water molecule can be seen poised for attack. The moment is technical, but the tension is human: the result depends on painstaking, exacting effort that can fail quietly, after weeks of careful handling, purification, and crystallization. Overseeing this work is Stephen Sprang, who has spent his scientific life inside problems where shape changes are the whole story. Earlier, as a structural biologist working on glycogen phosphorylase, he helped show that an enzyme can turn "on" through a movement measured in degrees: a small domain swivel, about 5°, that opens an active-site cleft and makes glycogen breakdown easier to trigger. Those structures, published in the late 1980s, helped establish him during a period when X-ray crystallography was transforming biochemistry from inference to image. By the mid-1990s, that same skill set is pulled into a new arena through collaboration with Alfred Gilman's group, where the question shifts from enzyme regulation to signaling, how a hormone's message becomes action inside a cell. The lab's discoveries arrive through other people's hands as much as Sprang's. That is the pattern the evidence keeps returning to: relationships that move ideas forward, and trainees who carry the work across the finish line. Years later, Sprang describes one of those trainees with a pride that is specific, not ceremonial: "Tesmer was among the most productive and creative postdoctoral fellows with whom I have had the honor to work. Indeed, I would say with confidence that he is currently the most productive structural biologist working in this area." That quote lands differently when you rewind to 1997, when John Tesmer, then a postdoc in Sprang's group, helps solve the first structure of a G protein bound to its target enzyme, adenylyl cyclase, capturing Gsα in its GTP-bound form with forskolin in the complex. In that structure, Switch II fits into a cleft between cyclase domains, and the active site shows a two-metal-ion mechanism for converting ATP to cAMP. It is an atomic-level view of a hormone signal being transduced. Here's the science, stripped to its moving parts. In the epinephrine pathway, epinephrine binds a β-adrenergic GPCR, which activates a G protein. The G protein's α subunit exchanges GDP for GTP, becomes active, dissociates, and stimulates adenylyl cyclase to make cAMP from ATP. Sprang's structural work matters because it does not just name those steps, it shows which loops shift position when GTP is present and how that rearrangement flips the catalytic switch. Sprang's adrenaline story feels like a quiet summary of the Dallas scene: crystals, metal ions, a fleeting transition state made visible, work that exists because knowledge and patience keep passing from one set of hands to the next.
