**Wylie Vale (1941-2012)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

Inside a temporary wooden barracks west of the main Salk Institute building, Wylie Vale and his team at the Salk Institute for Biological Studies are knee-deep in an industrial-scale nightmare. The air is thick with the overpowering, cloying stench of organic solvents and biological extracts. Vale oversees the processing of over 500,000 sheep hypothalami (the tiny, pea-sized base of the brain) which are being ground and washed in petroleum ether to find a single, mystery molecule. The smell is so pungent and persistent that it clings to their skin and clothing like a second layer. When they venture into La Jolla supermarkets, other shoppers physically recoil and hurry away from the smelly researchers.

The stakes of this odorous labor are great. Since 1948, when Geoffrey Harris first proposed that the hypothalamus controls the pituitary-adrenal stress axis, scientists have hunted for the specific factor responsible, but for decades it has eluded every attempt at capture. Vale, a former postdoctoral fellow of Roger Guillemin, is locked in an intense race against Andrew Schally's laboratory at Tulane, where being second means near-erasure from the discovery record. In this high-pressure environment, Vale maintains an uncompromising standard. When a colleague grows discouraged after a failed run of experiments, he tells her, "Effort only counts through the 3rd grade. After that, only results matter."

By spring 1981, the tension reaches a breaking point. A technician pulls an April Fools' prank, doctoring a fake journal table of contents to suggest the rival lab has scooped them. Vale sees through the joke almost at once, while a colleague nearly faints before catching on. The lab pivots back to its primary tool, a Beckman spinning cup sequencer, hunting for a specific chain of 41 amino acids amid staggering mathematical possibilities. Vale's team finally solves the sequence of corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF), a peptide that functions as a central switchboard for the body's stress response. When the brain perceives a threat, it releases this peptide, which travels to the pituitary gland and locks onto specific G-protein coupled receptors on the cell surface. This binding triggers a cascade that converts ATP into the second messenger cyclic AMP, ultimately signaling the body to release stress hormones like cortisol.

Vale's intuition for molecular architecture, perhaps inherited from his father, a prominent Houston architect, helps carry him from brute-force sheep extraction to precise engineering. He brings the same intensity to morning racquetball games on the Salk courts. As a young researcher he had once had himself injected with a hypothalamic hormone, TRH, to see firsthand what it did to the body. Later, whether founding biotechnology companies to turn peptides into treatments or mentoring the next generation of neuroendocrinologists, he held to the belief that risk was an essential ingredient of scientific progress.
