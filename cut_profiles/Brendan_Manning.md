**Brendan Manning (1969-)**

*Profile by an Anonymous Student, BIOL 3030, University of Virginia*

In 2002, the Department of Cell Biology at Harvard Medical School is a mosaic of fragmented pieces. Postdoctoral fellow Brendan D. Manning is hunched over a series of Western blots, strips of film showing the dark bands of proteins, trying to reconcile two worlds that refuse to touch. On one side is the PI3K pathway, a known driver of insulin response; on the other is mTOR, the engine of cell growth. Between them lies a gap that Manning suspects is filled by a protein complex called TSC.

The stakes are higher than mere academic ambition. For children born with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), the "brakes" on cellular growth have failed, leading to benign tumors throughout the body and progressive kidney dysfunction. Manning views these rare genetic malfunctions as a window onto much broader human biology. As he later put it, "I view TSC as a key linchpin disease, and how mTOR contributes to that." He has explained that "activation or inhibition of mTOR really changes the way that cells metabolize nutrients," a switch with consequences reaching well beyond TSC itself. Understanding the molecular logic that fails in TSC patients illuminates the pathways controlling metabolism in all humans, from normal growth to metabolic disease.

Manning's breakthrough lay in proving that the TSC complex is the direct molecular bridge. He demonstrates that a kinase from the insulin pathway, Akt, adds a phosphate group to the TSC protein. This modification acts like a gag, it stops TSC from performing its usual job of turning off a small protein called Rheb. With TSC silenced, Rheb stays active, constantly fueling the mTOR engine.

To understand the science, imagine a city's power grid. The PI3K pathway is the incoming electricity from a power plant, while mTOR is a massive industrial factory. Manning discovered that TSC acts as a "switchboard" or circuit breaker. When insulin or nutrients are present, the cell flips the TSC switch to "off," allowing the current to flow through a small starter motor (Rheb) that finally cranks the mTOR factory into gear to build new proteins and lipids.

By 2004, Manning moved over to the Harvard School of Public Health as the inaugural faculty hire for its newly created Department of Genetics and Complex Diseases (later renamed Molecular Metabolism). He arrived with no established lab culture, tasked with building a research program from zero. This transition reflected a pivot in his own scientific path. Years earlier at Yale, he had been a doctoral student in Michael Snyder's laboratory, studying the basic mechanics of mitosis and cell division in budding yeast, work with no obvious link to human metabolic disease until his postdoctoral years redirected him toward how cells sense nutrients and growth signals.

Today, Manning's workspace has shifted from the bench to the director's office, where he manages the flow of a new generation of researchers. He often reflects that the discovery of the TSC bridge wasn't the end of the story, but the beginning of a larger effort to understand why metabolic systems fail. By solving the mechanics of a rare genetic "switchboard," his lab searches for the secrets to more common metabolic crises, from diabetes to the rapid biomass accumulation of cancer cells.
